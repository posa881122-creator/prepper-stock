Step 1

Why prep?

Prepping is not waiting for the end of the world. It is having what you need at home when everyday life stops working, for a few days or a few weeks.

Help cannot reach everyone

In a large crisis, emergency services put those who cannot cope on their own first: the sick, the elderly and children. If your household can manage for a week, you free up help for them.

It has already happened

In April 2025 the power grid went down across all of Spain and Portugal. In many places the power was gone for hours, in some for almost a day. Storms, floods and winter weather cut power to millions of homes every year.

Everything is connected

Without power, far more than the lights stop working: the fridge, the heating, water in tall buildings, mobile networks, card payments and fuel pumps. That is why a flashlight is not enough.

Sweden and Norway ask every household to manage on its own for at least one week. Germany says ten days, the EU at least 72 hours. This guide helps you get there.

Crises we can learn from

2005

Storm Gudrun, Sweden

Hundreds of thousands of homes in southern Sweden lost power, some for several weeks.

Lesson: in rural areas, repairs can take a long time.

2021

Winter storm in Texas, USA

Nearly 10 million people lost power during a deep freeze. About half lost running water, on average for two days. The state counted 246 deaths.

Lesson: without power, heat and water go too.

2021

Cyberattack on Coop, Sweden

An attack on a software supplier knocked out the checkouts, and most of Coop's 800 grocery stores had to close for days.

Lesson: an attack far away can close the store around the corner.

2021

Ahr valley flood, Germany

134 people died in the valley in one night. Many were on the ground floor, and warnings did not reach them in time.

Lesson: heed warnings, and move up or out in time.

2025

Blackout in Spain and Portugal

The entire grid on the peninsula went down. Trains, traffic lights, payments and mobile networks stopped, in some places for almost a day.

Lesson: it can happen in a modern country, without warning.

Step 2

What happens when…

This is what happens in an ordinary home. Once you know what stops working, you understand why everything on the list is there.

  1. Right away
    Everything electric stops
    • Lights, stove, microwave, kettle and wifi.
    • Electric heaters, heat pumps and most boilers stop heating, even pumps in buildings with district heating.
    • Elevators, garage doors and electronic locks.
  2. First hours
    Your phone works, for a while
    • Cell towers have backup batteries that last a few hours, sometimes longer. Then it goes quiet.
    • Shops and gas stations cannot take payment or pump fuel without backup power.
    • The fridge keeps food cold for about 4 hours if you keep the door closed.
  3. After a day
    You notice the cold and the water
    • A half full freezer keeps for about a day, a full one about two.
    • In winter, homes get cold fast. A house without other heating can drop below 10 °C (50 °F) within a day or so.
    • In tall buildings and homes with a well, the water stops when the pumps stop. The toilet cannot be flushed.
  4. After three days
    What you have at home is what you have
    • Food in the freezer has spoiled. Shops have had few or no deliveries.
    • Phone and internet work poorly or not at all. Radio becomes the most important source of information.
    • This is when preparation makes the difference.
Most important: water, food that needs no fridge, a way to cook and stay warm, and a battery powered radio.
  1. Right away
    Payments and logins stop working
    • Cards, payment apps and digital ID can go down. Shops may take only cash, or nothing at all.
    • ATMs can be empty or out of order.
  2. Same day
    Public systems are affected
    • Pharmacies may struggle to see prescriptions. Hospitals may lose access to records.
    • Communication gets messy, and false information spreads fast.
    • Sometimes power, water or transport are hit too, because computers control them.
  3. Days
    Those with cash and food at home cope
    • Attacks can take days or weeks to resolve.
    • Trust official channels, not rumors on social media.
Most important: cash in small bills, cards from more than one bank, medicine for several days and important phone numbers on paper.
  1. During the storm
    Stay inside
    • Trees fall over power lines and roads. Power cuts are common, sometimes for days in rural areas.
    • In a flood, basements and ground floors can become unusable, and sewage can back up.
  2. Afterwards
    Roads may be closed
    • You may be cut off at home or unable to get home.
    • Drinking water can become contaminated. Follow local advice on boiling.
Most important: the same as in a blackout, plus valuables and documents kept high up and waterproof, and a packed bag in case you have to leave.
  1. Right away
    Nothing from the tap, or water you cannot drink
    • The cause can be a broken main, a power cut, contamination or sabotage.
    • The toilet cannot be flushed. Dishes and hygiene get hard.
  2. After a few hours
    Water distribution points open
    • It can take time, and you need something to carry the water home in.
    • If told to boil water: boil it until it bubbles strongly.
Most important: bottled water, empty containers with lids to fetch more, and trash bags for an emergency toilet.
  1. Heightened alert
    Society switches mode
    • Governments can declare a state of emergency. Those with a role in civil or military defense report for duty.
    • Goods can be hard to find, and power, transport and payments can be disrupted for a long time.
  2. When the alarm sounds
    Listen to the radio
    • Learn the public warning signal where you live. It usually means: go inside, close windows and listen to public radio.
    • During an air raid warning: take shelter in the nearest shelter, basement or other protected space.
  3. Afterwards
    Manage on your own for longer
    • You may need to manage without help for more than a week.
    • Be critical of information. False information is spread on purpose.
Most important: everything in this guide, plus knowing where your nearest shelter is and having passports, ID and important papers in one place.

How the list is calculated

Every item is chosen to keep for a long time at room temperature. Together the amounts give about 2,200 kcal per adult per day, with carbs, protein and fat within the Nordic Nutrition Recommendations. Children and the elderly are counted by their needs.

Step 7

Build your supplies in four weeks

You do not have to buy everything today. Take one week at a time, in this order. Buy a little extra every time you shop, and you will hardly notice it in your wallet.

Week 1

Information and water

Do: tune the radio to your local emergency station and test it. Fill clean bottles with tap water and write the date on them.
Week 2

Heat and light

Do: sleep one night in a sleeping bag and try the stove outdoors once.
Week 3

Food

Do: put the food on its own shelf. Buy one extra item every time you shop and eat the oldest first.
Week 4

Money, health and home

Do: talk to your neighbors about how you can help each other, and decide where you can sleep if your home cannot be lived in.
After four weeks you can manage for a week, as the authorities in Sweden and Norway ask. Want to go further: build up to two weeks, and pack the bags.

Sources: Swedish MSB, Sju dagar (2025). Norwegian DSB, self-preparedness for one week (2024). Ready.gov, Build a Kit.

Step 8

Seven areas, in this order

You can last a few days without water, a few weeks without food. That is why you start with water. The numbers in the lists are calculated for the household you entered.

1

Water

The most important of all, and what most people lack. Water takes space and is heavy, so start here.

0 L for your household
  • At least 3 liters per person per day for drinking and cooking.
  • Fill clean plastic bottles all the way with cold tap water and close the cap tightly.
  • Store dark and cool. Replace the water about twice a year and write the date on the bottle.
  • Put a few bottles in the freezer, not completely full. They keep food cold when the power goes, and become drinking water as they thaw.
  • If you are unsure about the water: boil it until it bubbles strongly.

Buy

Keep in mind: you also need something to fetch water in. Distribution points do not help if you cannot carry the water home.
2

Food

Food that keeps at room temperature, that you already eat and that can be eaten cold or cooked quickly.

0 kcal for your household
  • Buy what you already eat. Eat the oldest first and refill when you shop. Then nothing gets old.
  • Choose things that can be eaten cold or cook in a few minutes.
  • Small packs are better than big ones when the fridge does not work.
  • Keep dry goods in tight containers, cool and dry.
  • For long storage: keep dry goods like rice, oats, pasta, beans and sugar in Mylar bags with an oxygen absorber, heat sealed. This only works for dry food, not food with fat or moisture.

3

Heat and cooking

In winter, the cold quickly becomes the biggest problem. And without power you need another way to cook.

  • Gather everyone in one room, ideally the smallest, and close the doors.
  • Hang blankets over windows and doors. Put rugs on the floor.
  • Dress in layers and wear a hat. Sleep in a sleeping bag, ideally in a tent indoors.
  • Use camping stoves outdoors or with very good ventilation. Never use a grill or anything not made for indoor use inside, because of carbon monoxide.
  • If you have a wood stove: keep dry wood for the whole period.

Buy

Important: candles and indoor cooking are the most common cause of fires during power cuts. Have a battery smoke alarm and a fire extinguisher.
4

Light and power

It gets dark early in winter. And your phone needs charging so you can reach others.

  • A headlamp is better than a flashlight, it keeps your hands free.
  • Use batteries of the same size for everything, ideally AA.
  • Charge your power bank regularly. A full power bank charges a phone several times.
  • Save your phone battery: lower the brightness and use airplane mode when you are not using it.

Buy

5

Information

When the mobile network goes down, radio is how the authorities reach you.

  • Have a radio that runs on batteries, a crank or solar power.
  • Find out which station is used for emergency information where you live, and write down its frequency. In the US, NOAA Weather Radio also broadcasts alerts.
  • Learn your country's public warning signal and when it is tested.
  • Write important phone numbers on paper: family, neighbors, doctor, insurance.
  • Agree on where your family will meet if you cannot reach each other.

Buy

6

Medicine and hygiene

Pharmacies and care can be hard to reach. And without water, hygiene matters so nobody gets sick.

  • Medicines you take every day for the whole period, 7 days or more. Talk to your doctor if you need an extra refill.
  • First aid kit: band aids, bandages, pain relievers, a thermometer and what you usually use.
  • Emergency toilet: a bucket with a lid and heavy trash bags, for when the toilet cannot be flushed.
  • Wet wipes and hand sanitizer when there is no water to wash with.

Buy

7

Money and important papers

When cards and payment apps do not work, and if you have to leave home fast.

  • Cash in small bills, so you can buy food and fuel for a few days.
  • Cards from more than one bank, in case one bank is hit.
  • Passports, ID cards, insurance and prescriptions together in a waterproof folder.
  • Copies on paper, and also digitally in an app that works offline.

Supplies at home only help if you are at home. A packed bag means you can cope if you have to walk home, or leave home at short notice.

What your bag needs

Plan B: where can you stop?

If you cannot walk the distance in a day, you need somewhere to stop halfway, for example with a colleague, a friend or family. Decide in advance and tell your family.

  • Plan the way home in advance. Walk it once. Ideally choose two routes, in case one is closed.

Step 11

How to solve it

There is almost always more than one way. Here are the methods that work, what is good about them and what to watch out for. Choose how you live to see what fits you.

How do you live?

First: get water. Then: make it safe to drink. Clean tap water that you have stored does not need treating.

Store water in advance

Bottles and containers of tap water, in a dark and cool place.

  • Simplest and safest. Ready to drink right away.
  • Takes space and is heavy. Replace about twice a year.

Fill everything when it starts

If you hear of a disruption: fill the bathtub, pots, buckets and bottles while the tap still works.

  • Free and gives a lot of water quickly.
  • Bathtub water is for the toilet and dishes. Boil it if you need to drink it.

The water heater

In a house, the hot water tank often holds 100 to 300 liters (25 to 80 gallons) of clean water, which you can drain from the valve at the bottom.

  • A big reserve most people forget.
  • Turn off the power or gas to the heater and the main water valve first, so no dirty water gets in. Let the water cool.

The water in the pipes

When the pressure is gone, there is still water in the pipes. Open the lowest tap in your home and collect it.

  • A few extra liters, for free.
  • Close the main valve first if contaminated water could flow back.

Rain and snow

Collect rainwater in clean containers, or melt snow.

  • Often available when nothing else is.
  • Always needs treating. Water from roofs and gutters can contain dirt and bird droppings. Snow gives little water and takes a lot of fuel to melt.

Lake, stream or well

Fetch water from nature or a neighbor's well.

  • Unlimited, if it is close by.
  • Always treat it. Choose flowing, clear water, upstream from houses and farmland.

Boil

Boil the water until it bubbles strongly. This is what authorities advise when they issue a boil water notice.

  • Kills bacteria, viruses and parasites. The safest method.
  • Takes fuel and time. Does not remove chemicals, oil or heavy metals.

Water filter

Small filters you suck or squeeze water through, pump filters, or filters that drip by gravity.

  • Fast, no fuel. Removes bacteria, parasites and cloudiness.
  • Most do not remove viruses or chemicals. If the water may contain sewage: combine with boiling or tablets. Can be ruined by freezing.

Purification tablets

Tablets with chlorine or chlorine dioxide that you dissolve in the water.

  • Light, cheap and keep for a long time. Kill bacteria and viruses. Chlorine dioxide also handles most parasites.
  • Waiting time from 30 minutes to several hours. Tastes of chlorine. Works less well in cloudy and cold water.

Strain first

Pour cloudy water through a clean cloth or a coffee filter, or let it stand so the dirt sinks.

  • Makes filters last longer and tablets work better.
  • Does not make water safe. Always treat it afterwards.
Smart combination: store what you drink, fill the bathtub for the toilet, and keep both a filter and tablets as backup. Then you have two ways if one fails.

Eat in the right order: the fridge first, then the freezer while it is still cold, the pantry last. Open the fridge and freezer as little as possible.

Food that needs no cooking

Crackers, canned food, peanut butter, nuts, dried fruit and oats soaked in cold water.

  • Needs no fuel and no extra water. Works everywhere.
  • Boring in the long run. Hot food matters when it is cold inside.

Alcohol stove

A small stove that burns denatured alcohol. Common in hiking.

  • Simple, quiet and works well in the cold. The fuel is easy to store.
  • Slower than gas. The flame is hard to see in daylight. Use with good ventilation and never refill while it burns.

Gas stove with canister

A camping stove that screws onto a gas canister.

  • Fast and easy to control.
  • Pure butane works poorly below freezing. Choose winter blend canisters and keep them warm. Good ventilation.

Grill

A gas or charcoal grill, with a pot on the grate.

  • Many already have one. Handles big pots.
  • Outdoors only, never in a garage or under a roof. Carbon monoxide can kill. Check the rules for balconies where you live.

Wood stove or fireplace

Cook on top of the wood stove while you heat the house.

  • Heat and cooking at the same time.
  • Needs dry wood for the whole period and a clean chimney. Only works if the stove has a flat top.

Outdoor fire

A fire in a fire pit, a grill area or a safe open spot.

  • The fuel is often close by in the countryside.
  • Follow fire bans. Needs dry wood, kindling and practice. Hard in rain.

Hay box, saves fuel

Bring the food to a boil for a few minutes, wrap the pot in a sleeping bag, blankets or an insulated box, and let it stand. The food finishes cooking in its own heat. The method is also called retained heat cooking.

  • Saves a large part of the fuel. Works for rice, grains, beans and stews.
  • Takes longer. Does not work for pasta, which goes mushy.

The cold as a fridge

In winter, a cold balcony, a shed or a box outside can replace the fridge.

  • Free cooling for milk and leftovers.
  • Protect from animals and sun. Only works when it is cold outside.
Save fuel: lid on the pot, food that cooks in a few minutes, soak grains and beans beforehand, and boil only as much water as you need.

The first and cheapest step is always to keep the heat you have. A house without heating gets cold fast in winter.

One warm room

Gather everyone in one small room, ideally in the middle of the home. Close doors, hang blankets over windows and doors, put rugs on the floor.

  • Works everywhere and costs nothing.
  • Air out briefly a few times a day, or the air gets damp and stale.

A tent indoors

Put up a tent or build a blanket fort over a table, and sleep there in sleeping bags.

  • Body heat stays in the small space. Good for children.
  • Takes space. Never use candles or stoves inside the tent.

Dress right

Several thin layers, wool next to the skin, a hat and thick socks. Indoors and at night too.

  • The simplest thing there is, and it works.
  • Change into dry clothes. Damp makes you cold.

Hot bottles and hot food

Fill a thermos or hot water bottle when you boil water anyway. Eat hot food and drink hot drinks.

  • A lot of warmth for little fuel.
  • Use bottles made for heat, and wrap them so nobody gets burned.

Wood stove or fireplace

The best way to heat a house without power.

  • Gives a lot of heat for a long time. A masonry stove holds heat for many hours.
  • Needs dry wood, a clean chimney and a smoke alarm. Damp wood gives little heat and a lot of soot.

Propane heater

A heater that runs on propane and warms a room without power. Choose one that is made for indoor use and has an oxygen depletion sensor, which shuts the heater off if the air gets bad.

  • Always have a carbon monoxide alarm in the same room. Carbon monoxide cannot be seen or smelled, and people who breathe it in often notice too late.
  • Heats quickly without power. Many people already have propane for the grill.
  • Air out the room for a while every hour and never sleep with the heater on. Place it on a stable surface, well away from curtains and furniture, and keep a smoke alarm and a fire extinguisher nearby.
  • Many places only allow small cylinders inside homes and apartments, and large cylinders outdoors. Never store propane in a basement or a shared garage. Check the rules where you live.

Kerosene heater

A heater that burns kerosene with a wick and gives heat without power.

  • Always have a carbon monoxide alarm in the same room. Even a heater that burns well can fill the room with carbon monoxide if the air is not replaced.
  • Steady heat for many hours on one tank. The fuel is easy to store.
  • Use only the fuel the maker specifies. Refuel outdoors once the heater has cooled. Air out every hour and never sleep with it on.
  • Keep the fuel in its original container, and never in a basement storage room or attic.

Generator

A gasoline or diesel generator can run, for example, a circulation pump, a heat pump or a few heaters.

  • Can keep a whole house warm.
  • Always outdoors, far from windows, because of carbon monoxide. Uses a lot of fuel. Never connect it to your home's wiring without a licensed electrician.

Flower pot candle heater, a myth

Clay pots over tea lights that are said to heat a room.

  • Gives about as little heat as the candles alone, and can get hot enough to start a fire. Spend the effort on keeping heat in instead.
If you live in an apartment: your neighbors warm your walls. The room in the middle of the apartment stays warm longest. If you live on the ground floor, ask a neighbor higher up.

Use the same battery size for everything, and charge in advance. Most things can only be charged when you have power, not when it is gone.

Headlamp and flashlight

LED lights on standard batteries.

  • Safe, bright and the batteries last long.
  • Keep spare batteries of the same size. A headlamp keeps your hands free.

LED lantern

A lantern that lights up the whole room.

  • Better than candles. No fire risk.
  • Choose one that uses the same batteries as your lights.

Candles

Tea lights and candles.

  • Cheap and in most homes.
  • A common cause of fires during power cuts. Never unattended, never near fabric, always on a stable surface.

Power bank

A battery that charges your phone, lights and radio over USB.

  • Small and simple. A large power bank charges a phone several times.
  • Slowly loses charge. Charge it every month.

Portable power station

A large battery with regular power outlets.

  • Can run lights, a router, a charger or a small cooler. Quiet and safe indoors.
  • Not enough for heaters or a stove. Expensive.

Solar panel

A folding panel that charges a power bank or power station.

  • Gives new power for as long as the crisis lasts.
  • Gives little power in winter in the north and when it is cloudy.

Hand crank

Lights, radios and chargers powered by a crank.

  • Always works, without batteries.
  • Little power for a lot of cranking. Best as a backup.

The car

Charge your phone and power bank in the car.

  • A car is a big battery most people already have.
  • Outdoors only if the engine runs, never in a garage. Run the engine so the car battery does not die.
Save your phone: lower the brightness, close apps you do not need and use airplane mode. Text messages often get through when calls do not.

When the mobile network and internet are gone, you need to know where to find information in other ways.

Battery, crank or solar radio

How authorities reach everyone. Find out which station carries emergency information where you live. In the US, NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts alerts.

  • Works when everything else is gone.
  • Write down the frequency in advance.

The car radio

The car has a radio that works without the grid.

  • Most people already have one.
  • Do not run the engine in a garage. Without the engine, the radio can drain the car battery.

Local help points

Many towns open places in a crisis where you can get information, help and sometimes charge your phone.

  • Information and help when nothing else works.
  • Find out in advance where they are where you live.

Neighbors

Agree in advance who checks on whom, and who has what.

  • The fastest help is often in your building or on your street.
  • Think especially of elderly neighbors and those who live alone.

Paper and a plan

Important numbers, addresses and a map on paper, and a plan for where your family meets.

  • Works even when your phone is dead.
  • Update the list once a year.

Rumors and false information

In a crisis, wrong information spreads fast, sometimes on purpose.

  • Trust official channels. Ask who is spreading it and why, before you share it.
Learn the signal: find out what your country's public warning siren or alert sounds like, and what you should do when you hear it.

Without water, the toilet becomes the first problem. Good hygiene keeps everyone healthy when care is hard to reach.

Flush with a bucket

Pour a bucket of water quickly into the toilet bowl, for example bathtub water.

  • Simple if the sewer works.
  • In a power cut, sewage pumps can stop and the sewer can overflow. Flush as little as possible, and not at all in a flood or if authorities say so.

Emergency toilet

Put two heavy trash bags in the toilet bowl or a bucket with a lid. Sprinkle cat litter, sawdust or peat after each use.

  • Works when there is neither water nor sewer. The litter reduces smell.
  • Tie the bag when it is half full. Keep it sealed and cool until trash collection works.

Outhouse or pit

In the countryside, an outhouse or a pit far from wells and water can be a solution.

  • Lasts longer than bags.
  • Far from wells, lakes and streams. Cover after each use.

Wash with little water

A washcloth in a bowl of water goes a long way. Hands matter most.

  • Keeps you healthy with a few cups a day.
  • Always wash your hands after the toilet and before eating.

Wet wipes and hand sanitizer

When there is no water to spare at all.

  • Needs no water.
  • Hand sanitizer works poorly on dirty hands. Wipe off the dirt first.

Dishes without water

Paper plates, or wipe the pot clean with paper towels and wash when the water returns.

  • Saves water for drinking and food.
  • Wipe right away, before the food dries on.
Trash: trash collection may stop. Keep trash in sealed bags, cool and out of reach of animals.
Step 12

When it takes a little more

Those who depend on medicine, electricity or help from others are hit first and hardest. That is why the plan has to start here, in good time.

Daily medicine

Pharmacies and care can be hard to reach for days.

  • Keep at least a week of medicine at home, and your prescriptions on paper.
  • Ask your pharmacist how your medicine should be stored and how long it keeps without a fridge.
  • Talk to your doctor before getting extra. It does not apply to all medicines.

Equipment that needs power

For example oxygen, breathing support, feeding pumps or a power wheelchair.

  • Make a backup plan with your care provider in advance: what do you do when the power goes, and how long do the batteries last?
  • Keep spare batteries charged, and find out where you can charge in a crisis.
  • Tell your power company if you depend on electricity for medical equipment, and ask what to expect in an outage.

Elderly and living alone

Cold, darkness and isolation are most dangerous for those who are alone.

  • Agree with a neighbor or relative to check on each other.
  • Find out where your town's help points or warming centers are.
  • If you have home care: ask how they plan for a crisis.

Disability

Aids and communication must work even without power.

  • Keep assistive devices charged, with backup if possible.
  • Write down the key facts about your needs on a card, so others can help you.
  • If you have a guide or service dog: food, water and medicine for it too.

Children

Children cope better when they understand what is happening.

  • Talk about it calmly, in advance. Let them help pack.
  • Have games, books and something comforting, like a cuddly toy.
  • Keep routines for meals and sleep as best you can.

Babies and pregnancy

The youngest need clean water and warmth most of all.

  • Ready to feed formula needs no clean water.
  • Have diapers and clothes for the whole period.
  • If you are pregnant: keep the numbers to your midwife and maternity ward on paper.

    Step 14

    Common mistakes

    Things that are rarely in the brochures but that many beginners do.

    Emergency food you never tastedExpensive freeze dried pouches that nobody in the family likes. Start with ordinary food you already eat.
    Everything needs cookingPasta and rice need water and fuel. Have food that can be eaten cold, and expect the fuel to run out.
    The supplies get oldA pantry nobody rotates expires quietly. Write down the dates and eat the oldest first.
    Only food, no waterWater is what people lack most often. Start there, even if it feels boring.
    Everything at onceYou do not have to buy it all today. Buy a little extra every time you shop, and you have a week in a month.
    The phone as the only sourceWithout power and mobile network, a battery radio is the only thing that works.
